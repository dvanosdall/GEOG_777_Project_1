======================================================================
     REGRESSION ANALYSIS REPORT: NITRATE VS CANCER
======================================================================

Analysis Mode: CLEANED DATA
IDW Parameter: k = 2.0

RESEARCH QUESTION
----------------------------------------------------------------------
Do higher nitrate levels in drinking water predict higher cancer rates
at the census tract level in Wisconsin?

METHODS
----------------------------------------------------------------------
Analysis: Ordinary Least Squares (OLS) Linear Regression
Dependent Variable: Cancer rate (cases per person)
Independent Variable: Mean nitrate level (mg/L)
Unit of Analysis: Census tracts
Sample Size: 1261 tracts with complete data

Data Cleaning Applied:
  - Removed cancer rates > 100% (impossible values)
  - Removed negative nitrate (IDW extrapolation artifacts)
  - Removed extreme outliers (z-score > 3)

DESCRIPTIVE STATISTICS
----------------------------------------------------------------------
Nitrate (mg/L):
  Mean: 3.71
  SD:   2.55
  Range: [0.01, 11.83]

Cancer Rate:
  Mean: 0.0861
  SD:   0.1153
  Range: [0.0000, 0.5400]

REGRESSION RESULTS
----------------------------------------------------------------------
Model Equation:
  cancer_rate = 0.077245 + 0.002389 * nitrate

Coefficients:
  Intercept (Beta0): 0.077245
  Slope (Beta1):     0.002389 (SE = 0.001275)
  95% CI:         [-0.000112, 0.004890]

Model Fit:
  R-squared:      0.0028
  Adjusted R²:    0.0020
  RMSE:           0.115136
  F-statistic:    3.51
  p-value:        0.061158

STATISTICAL SIGNIFICANCE
----------------------------------------------------------------------
Result: NOT SIGNIFICANT (p >= 0.05)
Interpretation: Insufficient evidence of a relationship.
Cannot reject the null hypothesis.

EFFECT SIZE AND INTERPRETATION
----------------------------------------------------------------------
Direction: POSITIVE relationship
For every 1 mg/L increase in nitrate, cancer rate
increases by 0.002389 on average.

Variance Explained: 0.3%
Nitrate levels explain 0.3% of the variation
in cancer rates across census tracts.

Strength: VERY WEAK relationship

REGRESSION ASSUMPTIONS
----------------------------------------------------------------------
1. Linearity: Relationship between X and Y should be linear
   Check: Residuals vs Fitted plot

2. Independence: Observations should be independent
   Limitation: Spatial autocorrelation may exist (nearby tracts similar)

3. Homoscedasticity: Constant variance of residuals
   Check: Scale-Location plot

4. Normality: Residuals should be normally distributed
   Check: Q-Q plot and histogram

LIMITATIONS
----------------------------------------------------------------------
1. Ecological fallacy: Tract-level associations may not apply to individuals
2. Confounding variables: Other factors may affect cancer rates
3. Temporal mismatch: Cancer and nitrate data may be from different periods
4. Spatial autocorrelation: Violates independence assumption
5. Interpolation error: IDW introduces uncertainty in nitrate estimates

CONCLUSION
----------------------------------------------------------------------
No statistically significant relationship was found between nitrate
levels and cancer rates at the census tract level.
This does not prove no relationship exists, only that it was not
detected in this analysis.
